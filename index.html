§ 01

Approach.

Students learn science best when they are asking the questions — and trusted to follow them somewhere real.

For twelve years I taught science in urban, Title I high schools serving culturally, racially, and economically diverse students. My classrooms were calm and structured by design, with clear routines that put the science at the center — and they were mainstreamed, multi-level sections spanning a wide range of achievement, which is exactly the room I know how to teach.

I design lessons that work for that whole range from the outset: scaffolded investigations, multimodal representations, and daily partnership with special education staff, paraeducators, and instructional aides in co-taught classes. Supporting English language learners is not an add-on for me — it is the endorsement I hold and the population I have taught for over a decade.

Twelve years of dissection, microscopy, and wet-lab work without a safety incident. Over those years I taught four different science courses — Earth Science, Biology, Environmental Science, and Human Anatomy & Physiology — which means I can be placed where a master schedule needs me. As science department chair I guided an 18-member department through the district's NGSS transition at a time when no adopted curriculum existed, and I authored district-wide Earth Science benchmark assessments. As an early and deliberate adopter of AI in education, I use these tools to personalize learning — and I teach students to use them as a sharpener of their own thinking rather than a substitute for it.

i.

Inquiry & phenomena first

Every unit begins with a phenomenon students can observe, argue about, and explain — then the content follows the questions they raise.

ii.

Classroom management as care

Structured routines, predictable expectations, and warm authority create the conditions for students to take intellectual risks safely.

iii.

Equity in the design, not the patch

Lessons are built from the start to work for ELL, IEP, and 504 students — supports are structural, not retrofitted after the fact.

iv.

Safety is the precondition for rigor

Explicit protocol, modeled procedure, and constant circulation are what make ambitious lab work possible. Twelve years, incident-free.

v.

Data that changes Tuesday

Formative and benchmark data are only useful if they reshape the next lesson — and if families and administrators can see the same picture I do.

vi.

AI as a thinking partner

Students learn to use AI to strengthen their reasoning, check their work, and surface their own questions — never as a shortcut around the thinking itself.

§ 05

Teaching portfolio.

Four signature lessons that show how I teach — multi-day, firsthand, and built around students producing tangible scientific work rather than consuming content.

i. Human Anatomy & Physiology

Comparative Rat Dissection

A multi-day investigative lab in which students dissected preserved specimens to expose and study five organ systems — respiratory, digestive, circulatory, urogenital, and structural — then completed a structure-and-function analysis comparing rat anatomy to human physiology. Students compared male and female reproductive anatomy across specimens and produced a formal lab report.

PEs HS-LS1-2 · HS-LS1-3 · HS-LS4-1
SEPs Developing & using models · Constructing explanations · Communicating information
CCCs Structure & function · Systems & system models · Patterns
ii. Human Anatomy & Physiology

Histology Microscopy & Research Project

A multi-week independent research project using high-power microscopy. Students observed and sketched nine distinct tissue types from prepared slides — with teacher-verified specimen identification before sketching — and built a comparative structure-function-location reference for each using primary textual research, synthesized into a formal multi-section presentation and evidence-based written conclusion.

PEs HS-LS1-1 · HS-LS1-2
SEPs Obtaining & evaluating information · Developing & using models · Constructing explanations
CCCs Structure & function · Patterns · Scale, proportion & quantity
iii. Biology

Genetic Inheritance Lab: “Baby Face”

A multi-day investigative lab in which student partners simulated meiotic gamete formation through coin flips to model the inheritance of 23 traits in a hypothetical offspring, then synthesized their results into a drawn phenotypic portrait and a written post-lab analysis. The lab deliberately incorporated three distinct inheritance patterns — simple Mendelian dominance, incomplete dominance, and polygenic inheritance — and asked students to reason about probability and the limits of phenotypic inference.

PEs HS-LS3-1 · HS-LS3-2 · HS-LS3-3
SEPs Developing & using models · Using mathematics & computational thinking · Engaging in argument from evidence
CCCs Cause & effect · Patterns · Scale, proportion & quantity · Systems & system models
iv. Environmental Science · Cross-disciplinary

Biome Research Project

A multi-week role-based research project in which students investigated a single biome through six distinct scientific lenses — geographer, climatologist or oceanographer, botanist, zoologist, conservation biologist, and ecotourism guide — modeling how environmental science is conducted as an inherently interdisciplinary practice. Explicitly engaged biodiversity, organism-environment adaptations, climate change impacts, and real-world conservation strategy.

PEs HS-LS2-2 · HS-LS2-6 · HS-LS2-7 · HS-LS4-5 · HS-ESS3-1 · HS-ESS3-6
SEPs Obtaining & evaluating information · Constructing explanations · Engaging in argument from evidence · Asking questions & defining problems
CCCs Systems & system models · Stability & change · Cause & effect · Structure & function · Patterns
