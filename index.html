02

The room: calm, structured, and theirs.

My classes were mainstreamed and multi-level. The habits below are what held them together.

  1. Every class opens the same way

    A welcome, one shared breath, and a real question: how is everybody doing? Then I wait for the answer. Daily check-ins were my top priority.

  2. Names come first

    Learning each student's name, and saying it correctly, was a priority with every new class.

  3. Routines students can count on

    Clear expectations, structured collaboration, and guided discussion. I trained in Capturing Kids' Hearts, and the routines I built in my first years have anchored my teaching since.

  4. Built for the whole range

    Roughly half of my students were classified English learners, alongside students with IEPs and 504 plans. I scaffolded investigations, used more than one way to represent an idea, and co-taught daily with special education staff and paraeducators.

  5. Labs safe enough to be ambitious

    Explicit protocol, modeled procedure, and constant circulation. Twelve years of dissection, microscopy, and wet labs without a safety incident.

03

When students are struggling.

I'd rather find out on a Tuesday that a class is lost than find out on the unit test. This is how I found out, and what I did next.

  1. Small checks, every day

    Bell work to open, an exit ticket to close, and short quizzes in between. Frequent and low-stakes, so I had a current picture of who understood what.

  2. Reteach now, not after the test

    When those checks showed a class hadn't got it, I retaught it on the spot, before the gap had time to grow.

  3. English learners often told me first

    My English learners were often the first to signal what the whole class needed. I paid attention to that.

  4. Retakes that can only help

    Students could retake a test as many times as they wanted, and a retake could only raise the grade. I took that practice from the district's Grading for Equity work.

  5. Reading and math are part of science class

    I helped develop my school's numeracy and literacy plan for students reading and computing below grade level, the students for whom a science course is often where the gap shows.

05

Four lessons, rebuilt for NGSS.

I taught these before I was planning from NGSS. Each card shows the lesson as I taught it and how I'd rebuild it now: open with a phenomenon, give students a question to work on, and assess the explanation they build. It's the same work I'm doing on new lessons.

Biology

Genetic Inheritance Lab: “Baby Face”

As I taught it

Partners flipped coins to model which allele each parent passes on, across 23 traits, then drew the offspring's face and wrote a post-lab analysis covering simple dominance, incomplete dominance, and polygenic traits.

Rebuilt for NGSS
Phenomenon
Two siblings with the same two parents can look nothing alike.
Driving question
How do the same parents produce such different children?
What students do
Each pair runs the coin-flip model twice to make two siblings from the same parents. The class pools its data and compares the trait ratios it got with what probability predicts.
Evidence of learning
A claim, backed by class data, about where the variation comes from, and an account of where the model breaks down: most real facial traits involve many genes.

HS-LS3-2 · HS-LS3-3 · Arguing from evidence · Analyzing data · Cause and effect

Environmental Science

Biome Research Project

As I taught it

Over several weeks, students studied one biome through six roles: geographer, climatologist or oceanographer, botanist, zoologist, conservation biologist, and ecotourism guide. They combined their findings on biodiversity, adaptations, climate change impacts, and conservation.

Rebuilt for NGSS
Phenomenon
A local system under pressure that students can visit, such as the Duwamish River or the salmon ladder at the Ballard Locks.
Driving question
What would reduce our impact on this place, and what would it cost?
What students do
The six roles become expert groups gathering evidence on the same local system. Teams design a solution, weigh it against criteria and constraints, and revise it after critique from another team.
Evidence of learning
A design proposal that defends its trade-offs with evidence.

HS-LS2-7 · HS-LS2-6 · Designing solutions · Stability and change

Human Anatomy & Physiology

Comparative Rat Dissection

As I taught it

A multi-day lab. Students dissected preserved specimens to study five organ systems, compared male and female reproductive anatomy across specimens, and wrote a formal lab report comparing rat anatomy with human physiology.

Rebuilt for NGSS
Phenomenon
Sprint for thirty seconds, and your heart rate and breathing climb together.
Driving question
How do organ systems work together to keep one muscle cell supplied?
What students do
Use the specimen as evidence. They trace an oxygen molecule and a glucose molecule from outside the body to a leg muscle, and build a model of the systems that hand them off.
Evidence of learning
The annotated model, plus a prediction of what fails downstream when one system is compromised.

HS-LS1-2 · Developing and using models · Systems and system models

Human Anatomy & Physiology

Histology Microscopy & Research Project

As I taught it

A multi-week project. Students observed and sketched nine tissue types from prepared slides under high power, after I checked each specimen identification. They researched each tissue's structure, function, and location, then presented their findings.

Rebuilt for NGSS
Phenomenon
The lining of your cheek and the skin of your palm are both covering tissues, but only one stands up to friction.
Driving question
Can you tell what a tissue does from how it is built?
What students do
Start with unlabeled slides. They record what they see, predict each tissue's job and location from its structure, then check the predictions against research and revise.
Evidence of learning
Given a slide they haven't seen, students argue from its structure what it does and where in the body it belongs.

HS-LS1-2 · Constructing explanations · Structure and function

06

AI: boundaries first, then literacy.

AI is a thinking partner, never the thinker or the concluder. Students write their own questions and reach their own conclusions.

Commitment one

Dependency

AI can help a student practice or get unstuck. It can also wear down their ability to think for themselves. The questions and the conclusions stay with the student.

Commitment two

Desocialization

I won't let AI replace students talking to each other, collaborating, and building relationships. Any use of AI has to complement those human practices, not stand in for them.

Commitment three

Bias

AI can reinforce narrow perspectives, stereotypes, and existing inequalities. I won't delegate to AI the work of understanding another person. Students have to form that understanding themselves.

What I saw in Santa Ana

I never assigned AI tools. My students were using them anyway, mostly to get the work done for them. I talked with them about it, moved more of the thinking into the room where I could see it, and handled individual cases as they came up.

What I want for students

Students are growing up with these tools. I want them to learn, while they are still developing as thinkers, how to use AI without handing over the thinking: asking their own questions, checking what it tells them, and drawing their own conclusions.

Inside the district's rules

Any of this happens within Seattle Public Schools' policy and its list of approved tools. Where the district says no, the answer is no. When I'm unsure, I pause, ask a colleague, check district guidance, and pilot cautiously.

With colleagues

Teaching students to use AI well is something educators are all still working out. I've spent this year training in AI fluency, and I'd like to be a colleague who helps the team work out what good use looks like.

Three rules for my own planning

I run the lesson, and I redesign it afterward. AI can offer ideas. I'm the one who decides.

  1. I describe the task, where I need help, and what I bring to it.
  2. I ask for sources, then I check them myself.
  3. I ask who's missing, and I make it argue against itself.
